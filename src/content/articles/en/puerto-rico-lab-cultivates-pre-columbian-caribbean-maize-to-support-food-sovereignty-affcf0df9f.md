The Laboratory of Ethnoecology and Human Paleoenvironments at the University of Puerto Rico, Río Piedras, is cultivating “maíz caribeño precoz,” a pre-Columbian Caribbean maize variety. Seeds were planted in February 2026 at the Demonstrative Urban Garden of the Agricultural Extension Service at the university’s Botanical Garden; two months later, the plants stood five to six feet tall.

The laboratory’s stated goal is to return seed varieties such as this one to Puerto Rican agriculture to support food sovereignty and security in a climate-change context. It is also developing a Living Herbarium of Caribbean Ethnobotany and a social seed bank program, “Semillero social para el futuro.” In February 2026, Pagán Jiménez took part in a planting with farmers from Organización Boricuá de Agricultura Ecológica at Finca El Guaraguao in Orocovis, an indication of interest in conserving these seeds.

Pagán Jiménez said the maize was domesticated in southwest Mexico about ten thousand years ago and reached Trinidad and Tobago 7,800 years ago, and that Caribbean peoples contributed to its dispersal and adaptation. Starch-grain analysis on pre-Columbian teeth and ceramics, cited by Pagán Jiménez and including work by Reniel Rodríguez Ramos, indicates native Caribbean societies had complex, diversified diets and took part in plant domestication.

In Pagán Jiménez’s view, the pre-Columbian maize, adapted over hundreds or thousands of years, is better suited to Caribbean conditions than any hybrid developed in the twentieth century. However, the article provides no empirical agronomic data to support that comparison; the statement is an expert opinion.
