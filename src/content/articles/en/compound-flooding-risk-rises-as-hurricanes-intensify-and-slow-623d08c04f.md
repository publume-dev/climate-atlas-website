Hurricanes along most of the U.S. coast are projected to become 15–30 percent more intense and move 20–30 percent slower, according to a 2022 study by Gori and colleagues. That combination raises the chances that extreme rainfall and storm surge arrive together—compound flooding—which standard flood-risk assessments often underestimate because they evaluate surge and rainfall separately.

The shift would hit coastal communities unevenly. A 2023 study estimates that compound flooding made up 19 percent of Hurricane Harvey's flood area near Port Arthur, Texas; that share could rise to 33 percent under a plausible 2050 scenario with 0.57 meters of sea-level rise and 18 percent more rainfall, and to 46 percent under a 2100 scenario with 1.6 meters of rise and 50 percent more rainfall, with about 23 times more structures affected. In New York City, a Hurricane Sandy-like compound flood—now roughly a 150-year event—could become a one-in-65-year event by 2050 and a one-in-30-year event by 2100. Human-driven sea-level rise has already quadrupled the frequency of coastal sea-level extremes since 1900, the article reports, so a 100-year flood in 1900 was occurring every five years or more often by 2005 in places including Key West, Jacksonville, Atlantic City, and Maine. The article notes several studies without full bibliographic details, and the New York City projection relies on an emissions scenario described only as slightly higher than the current trajectory.
