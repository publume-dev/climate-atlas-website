A 2023 study found that 19% of Hurricane Harvey's flooding near Port Arthur, Texas, was due to compound flooding; under a 2050 scenario with 0.57 meters of sea level rise and 18% more rainfall, the compound flooding area would increase to 33%. A 2022 study by Gori et al. projected that, under an extreme global warming scenario for 2100, the return period for a 100-year storm surge coinciding with a 100-year rainfall would decrease by 7- to 36-fold in the South and 30- to 195-fold in the North.

The change affects coastal communities directly, as floods once considered rare are projected to become much more common. A 2024 paper found that a Hurricane Sandy-like compound flood in New York City is about a one-in-150-year event in the present climate, becoming a one-in-65-year event by 2050 and a one-in-30-year event by 2100 under a slightly higher emissions scenario. Regional drivers also differ: a 2022 study identified increased rainfall as the main driver of future compound flood risk along the Gulf of Mexico and Florida, increased storm frequency in the Southeast and mid-Atlantic, and sea level rise in the upper mid-Atlantic and New England.

Observed trends support these projections. A 2026 study found that human-driven sea level rise has quadrupled the frequency of coastal sea-level extremes since 1900, so that a 100-year coastal flood in 1900 had become a one-in-five-year or less frequent flood by 2005 in Key West, Jacksonville, Atlantic City, and Maine. In addition, a 2022 paper found that the initial threshold dates of continental U.S. named storm landfalls have trended earlier by two days per decade since 1900.

Projected return-period changes from the 2022 and 2024 studies carry considerable uncertainty because they depend on emissions scenarios and model variability. The source article presents median model results and a high-end scenario, so local risk estimates should be treated as indicative rather than exact.
