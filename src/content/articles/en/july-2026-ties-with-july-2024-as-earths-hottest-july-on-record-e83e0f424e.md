According to NOAA’s NCEI, July 2026 tied with July 2024 as the planet’s hottest July since record-keeping began in 1850. NASA rated the month as the hottest July on record, while the Copernicus Climate Change Service rated it the second-hottest.

Global ocean surface temperatures in July 2026 were the warmest on record, while global land temperatures were the third-warmest. North America, Africa, and Asia each had their warmest July on record. Loss data show the human and economic stakes: Gallagher Re cataloged 29 billion-dollar weather events worldwide in January–June 2026 with total costs of $104 billion, and Climate Central counted 12 U.S. billion-dollar weather and climate disasters in the first half of the year, causing at least $31.9 billion in damage.

Arctic sea ice extent in July 2026 was the sixth-lowest in the 48-year satellite record, and Antarctic sea ice extent was the fifth-lowest on record. For January–July, the global average temperature was the third-highest on record. NOAA’s Climate Prediction Center also updated its El Niño outlook, giving a 100% probability of El Niño conditions continuing through February 2027 and a 48% chance of very strong El Niño conditions during the August–October peak of hurricane season.

NCEI’s statistical analysis assigns a 99.9% chance that 2026 will finish among the four warmest years but a 0% chance of finishing first; the analysis does not account for El Niño. The article notes that a record-strong El Niño could still make 2026 the hottest year on record.
